Writing Kit

What it is
This kit gathers real, everyday English to teach Qwen, your own model, to write emails and other informal writing:
replies, requests, thank-you notes, follow-ups, scheduling, complaints, short announcements, text messages, memos,
chat messages, forum and talk-page comments, letters and public comments. It also keeps whole transcripts of real
speech (court arguments, hearings, parliament, everyday conversations) as colloquial English to learn from.
It makes training files of pairs: an instruction, such as "Write a short, polite email to a colleague declining
the meeting", and the writing that answers it.
It is separate from the picture training kit. It needs no graphics card and nothing to install except Python
(and, to read PDF files, the free pdftotext program from Xpdf or Poppler). To read SCANNED email releases (pictures
of printed pages), it also needs the OCR reader: see "Scanned email releases" below.

How to run it on Windows
1. Install Python 3 from python.org if you do not have it. Tick "Add Python to PATH" in the installer.
2. Open the writing-kit folder and press Enter on "Build Writing Data.cmd".
   This uses only the open and share-alike sources. It takes about 30 minutes the first time.
3. Or press Enter on "Build Writing Data with Personal Sources.cmd".
   This also uses the personal-use-only sources (see Licences below). It takes one to two hours the first time,
   because some sites are asked for one page every few seconds. A second build is quicker: downloads are kept
   in the "cache" folder.
4. When it says "Done", open the folder "out" in the writing-kit folder.
Each window says what it is doing, one line at a time, so your screen reader can follow it. Close the window to stop.

How to run it from a command prompt
Go to the writing-kit folder, then type one of these:
  python kit\build_dataset.py --out out --cache cache
  python kit\build_dataset.py --out out --cache cache --personal
  python kit\build_dataset.py --out out --cache cache --personal --per-source 1000
--per-source sets how many examples to keep from each source (300 if you leave it out).
--sources picks sources by name, separated by commas (the names are in the list of sources below).
--privacy strict uses the old, careful scrubbing instead of your rule (see Privacy below).
--enron-file uses a copy of the Enron file you already have instead of downloading it.
--oanc-file uses the Open American National Corpus file you downloaded in your browser (see below).

Settings for three sources (they stay in your computer, never in these files)
To add a setting: press the Windows key, type "environment variables", choose "Edit environment variables for your
account", choose New, and type the name and the value. Without a setting, that source is skipped and the build
says so.
REGULATIONS_GOV_API_KEY: a free key from api.data.gov, for the regulations.gov public comments.
SEC_USER_AGENT: your name and email address, for example "Your Name you@example.com". The SEC's EDGAR system
  asks every program to say who is asking, and refuses programs that do not. It is used only in requests to EDGAR.
The Open American National Corpus: its official download is on GitHub, and GitHub's robots.txt does not let
  programs download release files. So download OANC-1.0.1-UTF8.zip once in your web browser from
  anc.org/data/oanc/download/ (330 MB), and add --oanc-file followed by where you saved it, for example
  --oanc-file "C:\Users\You\Downloads\OANC-1.0.1-UTF8.zip".

Styles: training on one style at a time
Every example has a style label (one of the names below), a register (formal, neutral or casual) and its
source. The out folder has one folder per style, each with its own train.jsonl and validation.jsonl, besides the
combined train.jsonl and validation.jsonl, and STYLES.txt lists how many examples each style has.
To train on one style, use the files in its folder, for example out\email_business\train.jsonl.
To build only some styles, add --styles and the names, separated by commas, for example
  python kit\build_dataset.py --out out --cache cache --personal --styles email_business,memo,press_release
The styles:
  email_business: work email: requests, replies, updates, scheduling, follow-ups between colleagues or companies.
  email_personal: personal email to friends, family, neighbours or landlords.
  text_message: text messages (SMS) and very short notes.
  chat: live chat: IRC channels, customer service chats, assistant conversations.
  forum_post: posts on Usenet groups, mailing-list style forums and web forums.
  comment_public: public comments: comments to government agencies, Wikipedia talk-page comments.
  review: product and service reviews.
  social_post: short social media posts and announcements.
  letter_formal: formal letters: to agencies, the SEC, shareholders, fundraising letters.
  letter_personal: personal letters, mostly old public-domain letters.
  memo: memos and documents attached to work email.
  press_release: press releases from companies and agencies.
  report: reports from agencies and organisations.
  minutes: minutes of meetings.
  transcript_meeting: transcripts of hearings, parliament and public meetings.
  transcript_court: transcripts of court proceedings (US Supreme Court oral arguments).
  transcript_conversation: transcripts of everyday spoken conversations (telephone, face to face).
  speech: speeches, remarks and addresses.
  plain_language_gov: plain-language government writing: public notices and statements.
  legal: legal writing: opinions, filings.
  technical: technical and how-to writing, guides.
  academic: academic and textbook writing, grant summaries.
  news: news reports and magazine articles.
  blog: blog posts.
  fiction_prose: novels and short stories.
  fan_fiction: fan fiction released under an open licence.
  poetry: poems.
  screenplay_drama: plays and screenplays.
  dialogue: made-up dialogues between characters.
  children_story: stories for children.
  humour: humorous writing.
  nonfiction_prose: nonfiction books: history, essays, biography, travel and science (public domain).
  form_letter: form letters and notices from agencies to the public, in plain language.
  newspaper_news_historical, newspaper_editorial_historical, newspaper_ad_historical, newspaper_letter_historical,
    newspaper_notice_historical and newspaper_fiction_poetry_historical: historical US newspapers (news reports,
    editorials and opinion, advertisements, letters to the editor, notices and legal notices, serial fiction and
    poetry); see "Historical newspapers" below.

What you get, in the out folder
train.jsonl: the training examples, one per line.
validation.jsonl: about one example in twenty, kept back to check the training.
samples.txt: the first ten examples from each source, in plain text. Read this first.
build-report.txt: how many records were read and kept for each source, attachments and transcripts saved, and
  anything not used, with the reason.
ATTRIBUTION.txt: the credits and licences. Keep it with anything you make from the files.
sources-not-used.txt: the sources that were checked and cannot be used, and why.
messages folder: one folder per email that came with attachments, holding message.txt and the attachment files.
transcripts folder: whole transcripts of real speech, one plain text file each, by source.
audio_text folder: recordings with their matching text, for speech and text-to-speech work (see "Audio with
  matching text" below). These are not training examples for Qwen.
scanned-pages-left-out.txt: the scanned pages the OCR reader could not read well enough, with the reason (only
  when scanned releases were read).

What is in each example
instruction: what to write, with the tone and the audience. For a reply, the message being answered is included.
  For a chat, the conversation so far is included and it asks for the next message.
response: the writing itself.
messages: the same pair as a chat, user then assistant, the way most training programs want it.
style and register: the style label and formal, neutral or casual (see Styles above).
kind, form, tone, audience, length: what the rules found, for example kind "declining", form "email".
source, licence, licence_class, credit: where the example came from and under what licence.
The instructions are made from the writing itself by simple rules, with no AI and nothing online. For the
OpenAssistant and Dolly examples, the instruction is the request a person wrote.

Licences
Every source has one of three classes, the same as in the picture kit:
open: may be used and shared for any purpose, with credit. US federal government records are public domain.
share-alike: may be used for any purpose with credit, but anything made from it that you share must have the same
  licence.
personal use only: no licence lets others re-use it. Use it only to train your own model on your own computer, and
  do not share the files or the trained add-on. Corpora that are public but whose writers never gave up their
  copyright (the Enron emails, mailing lists, Usenet, IRC, public comments) are personal use only, even though
  anyone can read them, unless the source's own terms say otherwise.
Without the personal choice, the files have only open and share-alike examples.

Historical newspapers, 1770 to 1964 (added 22 November 2026)
What it is: American Stories, the articles of nearly 20 million pages of old American newspapers that the Library
of Congress scanned for its Chronicling America collection. Harvard researchers (Melissa Dell and colleagues, 2023)
found each article on each page and read it with OCR. It is formal and period English: news, editorials,
advertisements, letters to the editor, legal notices, serial stories and poems. Source name:
american_stories_newspapers. Licence: CC BY 4.0, open: use it for anything, with credit. The credit and the paper
to cite are written into out\newspapers\ATTRIBUTION.txt. No key or sign-up is needed.
The text is kept exactly as printed and read by OCR, mistakes and all: nothing is scrubbed, corrected or left out.
How big: 180 files, one for each year, 340 GB in all. The biggest years are around 1910, about 7 GB each; the years
before 1840 are small, and 1890 to 1919 are 187 GB together. The finished examples take about 190 more GB, so allow
about 530 GB of free disk space for everything.
How long: downloading all of it takes about 8 hours at 100 megabits a second, or about 30 hours at 25 megabits a
second. Building the examples afterwards takes about 10 hours when four years are built at a time (on a computer
with five or more processor cores), or about 40 hours one year at a time; one big year takes about an hour.
How to run it: press Enter on "Build Newspaper Data.cmd" in the writing-kit folder. It asks two questions:
  which years: press Enter for every year, or type years, ranges and decades separated by commas, for example
    1850-1869,1900s or 1865 or 1920-;
  the most gigabytes to download: press Enter for no limit, or type a number such as 50. The oldest of the chosen
    years come first, and it stops adding years before the limit.
It says how big your choice is and how long it will take, then each year's size as it downloads, with a running
total. You can close the window at any time: start it again and it carries on where it stopped, both the downloading
and the building. Finished files are checked and never downloaded twice. It stops before a year that would not fit
on the disk and says so.
From a command prompt, in the writing-kit folder:
  python kit\build_newspapers.py                                  (every year)
  python kit\build_newspapers.py --list                           (each year's size and what you have)
  python kit\build_newspapers.py --years 1850-1869,1900s --max-gb 60
  python kit\build_newspapers.py --download-only                  (download now, build later)
  python kit\build_newspapers.py --build-only                     (build what is downloaded; no internet needed)
  python kit\build_newspapers.py --styles newspaper_editorial_historical,newspaper_letter_historical
--folder chooses where the year files go (the folder "newspapers" if you leave it out); --workers sets how many
years are built at the same time (up to 4 unless you say).
What you get: out\newspapers has one folder per decade and in it one folder per year. In each year folder, each
style has its own two files, for example newspaper_editorial_historical-train.jsonl.gz and
newspaper_editorial_historical-validation.jsonl.gz. They are compressed; most training programs read them as they
are, and 7-Zip opens them. To train on an era, use the decade folders you want; to train on one style, use that
style's files. README.txt, NEWSPAPERS-REPORT.txt (how many of each style in each decade) and ATTRIBUTION.txt are in
out\newspapers.
Each example also has: year, decade, date, newspaper, state, page, headline, byline, ocr_quality and legibility.
ocr_quality is a number from 0 to 1: the share of the article's words that are ordinary English words (old OCR has
many misread words). legibility is how much of the article American Stories' own checker found clear (empty for the
earliest years). Nothing is left out for a low score, so your training can choose, for example only articles with
ocr_quality of 0.8 or more.
How the styles are chosen: the dataset does not say what kind of article each one is, so the kit decides by simple
rules on the headline and the first words: "Notice is hereby given", sheriff's sales and court notices are notices;
"To the Editor" or "Mr. Editor" are letters; "Chapter IV" or "To be continued" are serial fiction, and short lines
that nearly all start with a capital are poems; "Editorial", or phrases such as "in our opinion" and "it seems to
us", are editorials; "For Sale", "Wanted", prices and "call at" are advertisements; everything else is news. The
rules make mistakes now and then; the rule used is written in each example as style_rule.
In the ordinary build it is left out unless you name it, because it is so big. Adding --sources
american_stories_newspapers to build_dataset.py takes a small sample instead: 50 articles from each year you have
downloaded, or, if you have none, from the start of seven years spread from 1805 to 1955 (only the first part of
each file is downloaded, and it is not kept).
Checked and not built yet: the Library of Congress's own page-by-page OCR files (public domain, about 2.5 TB). Its
Chronicling America site is moving into loc.gov, it asks programs for 5 seconds between requests, and its text is
whole pages without articles. It could later add the pages American Stories missed; see sources-not-used.txt.

Privacy: your rule
You asked to keep everything: the full text of every message, people's names, signatures, the earlier messages
quoted in a thread, personal and sensitive subjects, and attachments. The kit does that.
There is one exception, and it is narrow. The kit still masks these, and nothing else:
- Social Security numbers, shown as [SSN];
- card numbers, shown as [card number];
- bank, account, routing and IBAN numbers, shown as [account number];
- passwords, PINs and login names written in a message, shown as [password] or [login].
Why: these add nothing to how someone writes, but a model can learn text by heart and repeat it later, and these
are exactly what someone needs to steal an identity or break into an account. Everything else stays as released.
A note about where the emails come from: the government email releases (Clinton, Bush, Palin and the like) were
reviewed and redacted by the agency that released them before they were published. The Enron emails were not:
FERC published them without redacting, so they contain private details of ordinary employees. The litigation
emails (Industry Documents Library, US v. Google) were produced in court and released under court orders and
settlements, sometimes with redactions. All of these are personal use only and should not be shared.
SCRUBBING.txt explains both settings in detail. If you ever want the careful setting back (names replaced,
sensitive messages dropped), add --privacy strict.

Attachments
Attachments are kept. For each email that had attachments, the kit saves the email as message.txt and every
attached file beside it, in out\messages\<source>\<message>\. Attachments that are written documents (plain
text, web pages, Word .docx, RTF, and PDF when pdftotext is installed) are also read, and their text becomes extra
examples, such as "Write the memo that was attached to this email". Old Word .doc files are saved but not read.
The Enron copy from Carnegie Mellon has no attachments: they were taken out before it was published.

Scanned email releases (OCR)
Many email releases are pictures of printed pages with no text: the Clinton State Department emails, Palin's and
Snyder's emails, agency FOIA releases and committee exhibits. The kit reads them with OCR (optical character
recognition) on your own computer. Nothing is sent anywhere, and it is not an AI vision model: it only reads letters.
The OCR reader is RapidOCR (Apache 2.0 licence). It was chosen because one pip command installs it, with its small
text-reading models inside, so it needs no separate program and never downloads anything while it runs. (Tesseract,
the other choice, needs its own Windows installer.) PDF pages are drawn with pypdfium2 (Apache 2.0 or BSD).
To install it once: press Enter on "Install OCR Reader.cmd" in the writing-kit folder, or type
  pip install -r kit\requirements-ocr.txt
It needs about 250 MB of disk space. Without it, the scanned sources are skipped and the build report says so.
Reading is slow: about two to five seconds a page on an ordinary computer.

Your own folder of scanned PDFs: --scanned-folder
If you downloaded releases yourself (for example with Program Downloader's personal assistant into
B:\Original sources), the kit can read them. Press Enter on "Build Writing Data from Scanned Folder.cmd" and type
the folder when it asks, or type:
  python kit\build_dataset.py --out out --scanned-folder "B:\Original sources"
Every PDF in the folder and its subfolders is read. Alone, --scanned-folder reads only your folder; add --sources
to read other sources in the same build, for example --sources scanned_federal,idl --personal.
--scanned-max-pages 200 reads at most 200 pages of each PDF (useful for very large releases).
What it does with each PDF:
- reads each page: a page with real text is read directly; a scanned page is read with OCR;
- keeps only pages the OCR is reasonably sure about (a confidence score of at least 0.80 out of 1, and at least
  8 words). The pages left out are listed with the reason in out\scanned-pages-left-out.txt;
- fixes common OCR mistakes in the header words ("Frorn;" becomes "From:", "Subiect" becomes "Subject:");
- removes release stamps, page numbers, Bates numbers and lone redaction codes such as B6 or (b)(6);
- splits the pages into emails wherever a From line is followed by Sent or Date and To or Subject lines, and joins
  a reply with the earlier message printed below it, so the example shows the message being answered;
- builds training pairs with the style labels, like every other email (most are email_business);
- saves each email's pages as pictures and their OCR text beside it, in out\messages\scanned_folder\. When the
  header lists attachments, the pages after the email are its attachment, and their text becomes an extra example.
Licence: your own downloads are personal use only, unless a PDF is a US federal government work, which is public
domain. The kit counts a PDF as a government work when its folder or file name contains "public domain",
"government work", "US government" or "federal", or when its first pages carry a US federal release stamp, a State
Department case or document number, or FOIA exemption codes such as (b)(6). If a stamp is too faint to read, put
the file in a folder named, for example, "public domain". Even then, a message whose sender's address is outside
the federal government (a company, a private address, a state office such as michigan.gov) is marked personal use
only. Each example's licence_class says which; ATTRIBUTION.txt gives the counts. State releases (Alaska, Michigan,
Florida) are not federal works, so they stay personal use only.
Privacy is your rule, as everywhere: only Social Security, card, bank and account numbers and passwords are
masked, in the text and as black boxes on the saved page pictures; everything else is kept.

Being a good guest on other people's sites
The kit reads each site's robots.txt and obeys it, leaves out any site that says no to AI training, waits between
requests (as long as a site asks, otherwise 3 seconds), and downloads only official files, pages and interfaces.
It never logs in and sends no personal details. API keys stay in your computer's settings, never in the files.
