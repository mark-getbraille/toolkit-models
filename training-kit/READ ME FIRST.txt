Picture Model Training Kit

The easy way
Download Picture-Model-Training.exe from the same page as this kit and press Enter on it: a wizard asks for the
folder and how much to train, prepares the computer, installs everything and trains, saying each step through
your screen reader. The rest of this file explains the same steps done with the .cmd files.
The program can also train a picture-making model (words to pictures, Stable Diffusion): choose it in "What to
train" at the top.

What it does
This kit teaches the Digital Accessibility Toolkit's picture model (the uncensored Qwen3-VL) to describe pictures
the way a blind reader needs. It learns from about 44,000 pictures that people have described, from these
collections:
- DOCCI: photos, each described carefully in about 120 words.
- VizWiz: photos taken by blind people, with one-sentence descriptions, and the questions blind people asked about
  their own photos with the answers ("I can't tell from this picture" when people could not answer).
- TextCaps and TextVQA: pictures with text in them (signs, labels, screens), described and asked about, so it learns
  to read text out.
- Localized Narratives: everyday photos that people described aloud.
- Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum, and the Art Institute of Chicago: descriptions their staff wrote for
  blind visitors and screen-reader users.
- The Metropolitan Museum of Art: its best-known works, learned by name ("What artwork is this?").
- Europeana: objects from museums and archives across Europe, learned as "Tell me about this museum object".
- NASA: space, Earth and aviation pictures with NASA's captions.
- COCO and Crossmodal-3600: everyday photos from around the world, each described in one sentence (only photos whose
  own licence allows it).
- Canterbury Museum and Auckland Museum (New Zealand): their staff's descriptions of how objects and old photographs
  look.
- Wellcome Collection (UK): prints, drawings and photographs, learned by their descriptive titles.
- Ready-made packs, built on GitHub and downloaded from the kit's own repository: the Smithsonian museums'
  accessibility descriptions and gallery labels, questions about everyday photos answered with the reason why,
  Wikimedia Commons' best pictures with their captions, and Te Papa (Museum of New Zealand) records.
- Wikipedia (only with "Start Training with Wikipedia"): pictures with the alt text Wikipedia's writers wrote for
  screen-reader users, from two collections (WIT and Concadia).
Two kinds of pictures are your choice (check boxes in the program; --with-nudity and --with-medical with the .cmd
files): art with nudity (paintings, drawings, sculptures and photographs from museums showing nude people, described
plainly; nothing sexual and no children), and medical and anatomy pictures (anatomy textbook figures and old
anatomy plates). For a picture describer both are ticked at first, so it can describe such pictures plainly
instead of refusing; for a picture-making model neither is. The report says which were used.
Graphic scenes (war, disasters, injuries and death, described plainly, and the dangers around someone caught in a
war zone or a disaster: rubble, fire, floods, fallen power lines, warning signs): when you tick medical and anatomy
pictures for a picture describer, the program asks "Also include graphic scenes: war, disasters, injuries and death,
described plainly?" (its box starts ticked; Alt+G is the box, Alt+O OK, Alt+C Cancel, which leaves them out).
With the .cmd files: --with-graphic. Nothing sexual is included, nor pictures whose focus is a dead or injured
child. A picture-making model never uses graphic scenes. The report says whether they were used.
Your own pictures: you can also teach it with your own described pictures, for personal use. Read "HOW TO ADD YOUR
OWN PICTURES.txt" in this folder.
Describing pictures with friends: the program's "Describe pictures" button (Alt+J) lets you and your friends
describe pictures together, for example stills from your own videos, and pass the work between you as zip files
by USB stick, email or a shared drive, with no website. Read "HOW TO DESCRIBE PICTURES WITH FRIENDS.txt".
Preparing pictures for describing: the second program, Dataset-Preparation.exe (next to Picture-Model-Training.exe),
takes good stills out of videos, removes date stamps and logos, sets aside dark, blurry and repeated pictures, and
has two or three picture models write draft descriptions, so the person describing starts from a draft. Run it
when no training is running. Read "HOW TO PREPARE PICTURES FOR TRAINING.txt".
It trains a small add-on to the model, then tests whether the model really got better. The model itself is not
changed. Nothing is sent anywhere; the kit only downloads what it needs. If one collection's website is down, the
kit says so and carries on with the others.

Personal-use collections
Some picture collections are free to download but not openly licensed: their licences forbid commercial use (for
example CC BY-NC), or their terms allow only research or "academic" use. The program has two check boxes for them,
both off at first, remembered separately for a picture describer and a picture-making model:
- "Include non-commercial collections (personal use only)" (Alt+Q).
- "Include research-only collections (personal use only)" (Alt+Z). Ticking it opens a window listing each
  research-only collection, its terms page and its terms in one line. Read them, tick "I have read these terms and
  will use the result only for personal, non-commercial purposes" (Alt+I) and press Agree (Alt+A). Cancel or Escape
  unticks the box. With the .cmd files: --personal-noncommercial, and --personal-research --accept-research-terms.
When either is ticked, the program says "This training will be for personal use only." The result is for you alone:
do not sell it, publish it, put it in a product or share it outside personal, non-commercial use. Its name ends in
"-personal", its report starts with a PERSONAL USE ONLY warning listing those collections and their terms, and its
zip holds PERSONAL-USE-ONLY.txt. A follow-up training only carries on from a personal-use training when you tick
the same box again.
Most collections that need a sign-up or a signed agreement (for example MIMIC or ImageNet) cannot be downloaded
by the kit (the few it can are below). If you got such a collection yourself, and its terms allow it, you can put its pictures with their descriptions
into your own pictures folder (see HOW TO ADD YOUR OWN PICTURES.txt); the result is then for personal use only too.

Personal use and sign-up collections
"Personal use: everything allowed" (Alt+1) is one check box that ticks every collection you may use for your own,
private training: the share-alike ones (Wikipedia), the non-commercial ones, the research-only ones, and the sign-up
ones whose key or files are on this computer. Before it ticks anything, a window says what it means and how much it
downloads in all, and lists the research-only terms. Read it (Alt+A), tick "I understand" (Alt+I) and press OK
(Alt+O); Cancel (Alt+C) or Escape changes nothing. What it means: the model is for your personal, private use only.
Never publish it, share it, sell it or put it in a product; the licences of the non-commercial and research
collections forbid selling the model or anything it makes. It does not tick art with nudity, medical pictures,
graphic scenes or adult sexual content: those keep their own questions. For a picture-making model, collections of
people and other collections not allowed for picture-making are still left out. Unticking it puts the three boxes
back as they were. The program remembers it.

Sign-up collections need your own free account. Each one, the first time it runs, says where to get its key, the
line of the terms you agree to, and what it adds; without its key it is left out and the training carries on.
- DVIDS (US forces at work, and Canadian Armed Forces photos): DVIDS_API_KEY. Sign up at https://api.dvidshub.net
  (Sign up at the top right), then create an API application; its key starts with key-.
- Kaggle Chess Positions (chess diagrams read out square by square): KAGGLE_USERNAME and KAGGLE_KEY. Make a free
  account at https://www.kaggle.com , open Settings, API, Create New Token. The kaggle.json file it gives holds both;
  you can also simply keep that file in the .kaggle folder of your user folder.
- Flickr30k (everyday photos described by people): no key. Fill in the request form at
  https://forms.illinois.edu/sec/229675 . Put the files you receive (flickr30k-images.tar.gz and flickr30k.tar.gz) in
  Documents\Picture Model Training sign-up files\flickr30k.
- Museums Victoria (household appliances, radios, tools): no key; it downloads slowly on your computer and says so
  plainly if the museum refuses your computer.
- Collections you download by hand (no key): you get the files from the collection's own page, after signing up or
  accepting its terms, and put them, as zip files or unpacked, in Documents\Picture Model Training sign-up
  files\<name>. The first time each runs, it says which files and from where. They are: lisa_traffic_lights (traffic
  light colours, Kaggle), lisa_traffic_signs (US road signs, UC San Diego), bdd100k (driving photos with weather and
  traffic lights, Berkeley), mapillary_signs and mapillary_vistas (street signs and street scenes, Mapillary),
  deepfashion (kinds of clothing), fastener_sets (bolts, screws, nuts and washers sorted by kind, from Kaggle or
  Roboflow) and mvtec_d2s (supermarket products). A .tar or .tar.xz file must be unpacked first (7-Zip does it).
- Keys the kit already uses: EUROPEANA_KEY (https://pro.europeana.eu/page/get-api), DPLA_API_KEY
  (https://pro.dp.la/developers/api-basics) and SI_API_KEY (https://api.data.gov/signup/).
Two ways to give the program a key:
1. In the "Personal use: everything allowed" window press "Sign-up keys" (Alt+K), type each key in its field and
   press Save (Alt+S). They are kept only in your own settings folder on this computer
   (%APPDATA%\Picture Model Training\signup-keys.json), never in the training folder and never in the log.
2. Or as a Windows environment variable for your account: press Start, type "environment variables", choose "Edit
   environment variables for your account", press New, type the name (for example DVIDS_API_KEY) and the key, and
   press OK twice. Close and open the program again so it sees the new variable.
Warning: never paste a key into a chat, an e-mail, a forum or a support message. Anyone with your key can use your
account. If a key was shared by mistake, make a new one on the site and delete the old one.

Review collections
Some picture collections are kept for a blind adult's own private describer only: medical pictures of adult
genitals and breasts (healthy bodies, normal variation and common conditions), skin conditions on all skin tones,
and, separately, adult sexual content (artworks, sex education and the like). They are never trained on as they
come. In the program, press Describe pictures (Alt+J), then Review collections (Alt+Y). Tick the collections to
download with Space, tick "I will have every picture screened by a trusted sighted adult before training" (Alt+W)
and press Download into a review workspace (Alt+D). A research-only or non-commercial collection shows its terms
first, and you must agree. Each collection goes into the Review folder of your picture library.
A trusted sighted adult then opens each collection (Open in Describe pictures, Alt+O), deletes anything that should
not be there, corrects each description and marks a picture checked only when it is right. ONLY CHECKED PICTURES ARE
EVER USED. A description that still says <description needed> is never used, and a package from a friend can never
mark a picture checked for you.
When you train a picture describer, the program uses the checked pictures of every review collection by itself, as
your content choices allow: medical pictures need "Include medical and anatomy pictures"; intimate pictures need
both that and "Include art with nudity"; adult sexual content needs art with nudity and a yes to the question that
appears when you tick art with nudity ("Also include adult sexual content from collections you have reviewed?").
Research-only and non-commercial collections also need their personal-use box. A training with any review picture
is for personal use only. A picture-making model never uses review collections, intimate or sexual pictures.
Some collections are used only with an organisation's permission: the list says "Permission on file" or "Waiting for
permission". Put their reply in the Permissions folder of your picture library (the list names the folder). Only
then can it be downloaded, or what they sent imported (Import what they sent, Alt+I). The report names the
organisation and says "used with permission, personal use only".

What you need
- An NVIDIA graphics card with at least 12 GB of memory for the 4B model, or 16 GB for the 8B model, with an
  up-to-date NVIDIA driver.
- About 90 GB of free disk space (110 GB for the 8B model) on the drive where you unpack the kit.
- An internet connection for the downloads, about 40 GB in all (more with Wikipedia).
- Time: the downloads take a few hours, and training on all the pictures takes roughly a day on a fast card.
  "Quick Try" uses a tenth of the pictures and takes an hour or two.

How to start
1. Unpack this zip to a folder on a drive with enough free space, for example D:\Training. Do not run it from
   inside the zip.
2. Press Enter on "Prepare This Computer.cmd" once and answer Yes when Windows asks for administrator permission.
   It stops sleep and hibernation, pauses Windows Update for 7 days, allows long file names and asks Windows
   Defender not to scan the kit folder. "Restore Normal Settings.cmd" puts it all back after the training.
3. Start with "Quick Try.cmd" (press Enter on it) to check that everything works on your computer. Then press Enter
   on "Start Training.cmd" for the real training of the 4B model. Pictures already downloaded by the quick try are
   used again.
   Other choices:
   - "Start Training Full.cmd": up to three times as many pictures from every collection (about 110,000), for the
     best result if the computer can run for several days (roughly three days on a fast card).
   - "Start Follow-up Training.cmd" (or "Follow-up" in the program): after a training has finished, this carries
     on from its add-on with only the collections that are new since then (new packs, the health collection, the
     describers' website), mixed with some of the earlier pictures so nothing is forgotten. A few hours. Its report
     compares it with the earlier add-on on every collection.
   - "Start Training 8B.cmd": the 8B model (only with a 16 GB card or bigger; do the 4B model first).
   - "Start Training with Wikipedia.cmd": also learns from Wikipedia. Wikipedia's text is under the CC BY-SA 4.0
     licence, so this add-on must be shared under CC BY-SA 4.0 too. It is published as a separate choice; the
     normal add-on stays under a permissive licence.
4. A console window opens. It says each step in plain words; JAWS and NVDA read it. Every line also goes into the
   file training-log.txt in the kit folder, which you can open at any time.
   Computer use: at the start the log describes the computer (processor, memory, graphics card and driver, disks,
   Windows power plan). Every 5 minutes it adds a "Computer use" line covering:
   - how busy the processor was (and its busiest core);
   - the processor-area temperature, when Windows gives it;
   - the memory used;
   - the graphics card: how busy, its memory, temperature, power, speed, fan, and anything slowing it down;
   - each disk's read and write speed, and the download speed.
   During training, a line every 200 pictures also says where the time went: preparing pictures on the processor,
   work on the graphics card, or saving. "Computer use warning" lines are spoken, for example when the graphics card
   is very hot or memory is nearly full. Every 15 seconds a row is also added to a spreadsheet, "Picture Model
   Training computer use <date>.csv", in the "Picture Model Training logs" folder on the desktop. It opens in
   Excel, for finding what slows the training down.
5. Leave the computer on. The kit stops Windows from going to sleep while it runs; the screen may turn off.

The steps it says
1. Checking the graphics card and the disk.
2. Downloading the model (about 9 GB for 4B).
3. Downloading the pictures of each collection and making them smaller. It says each collection's name and how
   many pictures are ready.
4. Testing the model before training (about half an hour).
5. Training: the long part. It says how many pictures are done and how long is left every 200 pictures.
6. Testing the trained model on the same pictures.
7. Writing the report and the file to send, with the toolkit version of the add-on in it.

If it stops
You can close the window at any time, or the computer can restart. Press Enter on the same Start file again: it
carries on where it stopped. Training carries on from its last save; a collection already downloaded is not
downloaded again. If it says STOPPED, it gives the reason on the same line, for example a card with too little
memory or too little disk space.

When it has finished
It says FINISHED and whether the model got better. Two files matter:
- The report (for example report-4b.txt) in the kit folder: the scores before and after for each collection, and
  examples, each with the question, a person's answer, and the model's answer before and after training.
- The zip in the "result" folder (a few hundred MB): this is what to send. Tell Claude the training has finished
  and you will be told how to send it. The add-on is only published in the toolkit if the tests show it is better.
  With the program, the result is in "<training folder>\Picture Model Training Kit\result", named like
  picture-training-4b-full-2026-11-18.zip (the model, the kind of training and the date). "Open the result folder"
  (Alt+F in the program) opens it.

Using your describer in the Digital Accessibility Toolkit
You do not need to wait for the add-on to be published: the result zip already holds a version the toolkit can use.
Inside the zip:
- adapter: the add-on as it was trained (for sending).
- toolkit: the same add-on made for the toolkit (describer-addon.gguf, in the format of llama.cpp, the engine the
  toolkit uses to describe pictures), addon.json (what it is, for the toolkit), and HOW TO USE THIS IN THE
  TOOLKIT.txt.
- report.txt, scores.json, ATTRIBUTION.txt (credits and licences) and training-log.txt.
To install it: open the Digital Accessibility Toolkit, go to the Describe pictures settings, choose "Install a
trained describer..." and choose the zip itself (not a file inside it).
To use it on another computer: copy the zip to that computer (USB stick, shared drive or email) and install it there
the same way.
It works only with the toolkit's matching picture model, Qwen3-VL 4B for a 4B training and Qwen3-VL 8B for an 8B
training. A training with your own pictures, personal-use or review collections is for your personal use only, and
one with Wikipedia is under CC BY-SA 4.0; the toolkit folder's text file says so too.
If the toolkit version could not be made (the window says "The toolkit version of the add-on could not be made"),
the zip is made without it: start the kit again, and it tries again before anything else. A zip made by an older kit
without the toolkit version also gets it at the next start, once, as long as the "work" folder is still there. To do
it without starting a training, press Enter on "Make Toolkit Add-on.cmd" in the kit folder. Making it downloads
llama.cpp's converter once (about 40 MB, from GitHub); nothing is sent anywhere.

Topic add-ons
A topic add-on gives the picture describer more expertise in one subject, such as medicines or food. Qwen stays
your general describer for everything; you choose which topic add-on to load in the toolkit, when you want it, and
you can train as many topics as you like, one at a time.
The topics:
- Medicines and medical supplies (medicines): pills and their imprints, blister packs, medicine labels, bandages,
  syringes, inhalers, instruments, animal medicines.
- Drugs and paraphernalia (drugs): street drugs, counterfeit pills, seizures, pipes, bongs, vapes.
- Firearms and accessories (firearms): guns by kind, maker and model, cartridges, holsters, scopes, sights.
- Food and packaged goods (food): fresh or spoiled food, groceries, packaged goods, use-by dates, text on packs.
- Drinks (no alcohol) (drinks): soft drinks, energy drinks, juices, water, iced tea and coffee drinks, plant milks.
- Alcoholic drinks, brewing and distilling (alcohol): wine, beer, spirits, cocktails, glassware and bar tools, and
  brewing, winemaking and distilling (with the legal and safety notes).
- Vehicles, machines and devices (machines): vehicle models, car and machine parts, dashboard symbols, tools,
  plugs, clocks, displays, electronic parts and repair, assistive devices.
- Home and household (home): appliances and their controls, kitchens, laundry and care labels, cleaning products
  and hazards, home repair, locks, heating and emergency lighting, building work and ramps, pests, baby products,
  sewing and crafts, first aid, dental and eye care, and emergency kits, warnings, shelters and disasters.
- Power and electrical (power): generators and their safety labels, solar panels, charge controllers and
  connectors, battery banks, wind turbines, water wheels and turbines, fuel, power lines, and home wiring, outlets,
  breakers, plugs, cables and circuits (with the carbon monoxide, back-feeding, solar and battery safety notes).
- Radio communications (radio): ham, GMRS, FRS, CB, marine and scanner radios, weather radios, antennas, towers,
  connectors and meters, Morse keys, ham stations, QSL cards and frequency charts, and off-grid mesh and data radio
  (Meshtastic, LoRa, APRS).
- Privacy, security and digital safety (privacy): security keys, code tokens, routers, home cameras and webcam
  covers, sign-in and padlock screens, password and authenticator apps, and made-up scam texts, phishing emails and
  fake pop-ups with their warning signs.
- Art and museum objects (art): museum descriptions written for blind visitors, famous works, instruments, crafts.
- Documents, maps and signs (documents): letters, forms, receipts, tables, charts, maps, sheet music, road and
  safety signs, phone screens.
- Plants, animals and nature (nature): plants, animals and breeds, crops and their diseases, weather, landscapes.
- Blind and low-vision living (vision): braille and braille writers (Perkins Braillers), slates, white canes,
  guide dogs, tactile paving and graphics, braille signs, reading machines, magnifiers, eye charts, sighted guiding,
  deafblind communication, money by touch.
- Nudity in art and photography (nudity): sensitive. Nudes in art and non-sexual photographic studies of adults.
- Sexual content (sexual): sensitive, and always for personal use only. Erotic art showing adults, and checked
  pictures of your adult review collections.
How to train one in the program:
1. On the first page, choose the folders, the model and How much to train as usual (a quick try, normal or full;
   not a follow-up). Leave What to train on A picture describer.
2. Press "Topic add-ons" (Alt+2). A window opens.
3. In Topic (Alt+T), choose a topic with the arrow keys. Its name and licence are said as you move.
4. Read About this topic (Alt+A): what the add-on knows, how many of its collections are used, which are left out
   until a box is ticked on the first page, and its licence.
5. Everyday share (Alt+E) is how much of the training is ordinary pictures, so the add-on does not forget how to
   describe everyday things. 15 percent is a good start; you can choose 0 to 40. If the report says everyday
   description got worse, train again with a higher share.
6. Press "Start training this add-on" (Alt+S). Cancel (Alt+C) or Escape closes the window without training.
   "Test on real questions" (Alt+Q) tests the topic instead of training it (see Testing on real questions below).
The training then runs like any other, saying each step. Some collections of a topic are share-alike,
non-commercial or research-only: they are used only when Include Wikipedia, or the non-commercial or research-only
box, is ticked on the first page. The nudity and sexual content topics tick the content choices they need by
themselves. Your own pictures are not used for a topic add-on.
The report starts with the topic's score in plain words: how the add-on did on the topic's own test pictures, which
were kept back and never trained on, and on everyday test pictures, each before and after training (from 0 to 1;
higher is closer to people's own descriptions).
It also says how the add-on did on blind people's real questions of that topic and on everyday ones, before and
after training (see Testing on real questions below).
The result zip is named after the topic, for example picture-training-4b-topic-food-2026-11-20.zip. Install it in
the toolkit the same way as any trained describer. Inside it, addon-label.json says which topic it is, which model
it is for, whether it is sensitive, and its licence: "open", or "personal use only" when it learned from any
non-commercial or research-only collection, review pictures or sexual content. A personal-use add-on must never be
published, shared or sold. A topic add-on only describes pictures; it is never used for making pictures. The label
is explained in "TOPIC ADD-ON LABEL FORMAT.txt".
With the .cmd files: open a command prompt in the kit folder and type, for example, kit\run.cmd --model 4b --topic
food (add --topic-replay 0.25 for a 25 percent everyday share; kit\run.cmd --topic list says the topics).

Testing on real questions
The kit has a test made of questions that blind people really asked about their own photos, to see whether a topic
add-on (or any trained describer) really helps. There are 600 questions, about 50 for each topic plus everyday ones:
medicines 50, drugs 22 (cigarettes and tobacco), food 50, drinks 50, alcohol 50, machines 50, art 35, documents 50,
nature 50, home health tests 21 and everyday 172. Firearms, nudity and sexual content have none: no open set of
blind people's questions has them. Home health tests are questions about reading a pregnancy test's result lines;
COVID-19 and ovulation tests are not in any open set yet. They are the questions blind people ask every day: what
is this, read the label, the expiry date, how much, what colour, which button, is it on or off, which banknote, and
which medicine.
Where they come from, all under the CC BY 4.0 licence (credited in every report):
- VizWiz (vizwiz.org): photos blind people took with their phones, each with their question and the answers of ten
  sighted people. Mostly from its test part, which the kit never trains on. Some are photos with private information
  (bills, cards, mail, prescription labels, screens) that VizWiz published with the private parts covered over; only
  those covered-over copies are used. Questions about passwords, identity numbers, faces, tattoos, number plates,
  nudity or anything with a phone or card number in the answers are left out.
- ORBIT (City, University of London and Microsoft): frames of videos blind people filmed of their own things, with
  "What is this?" and the name they gave the thing.
These photos are never trained on: a training leaves them out by itself.
The list of questions is in the kit (kit\real_questions.jsonl, small). The photos, about 200 MB, are downloaded the
first time on this computer, from VizWiz's own server and ORBIT's own download (one frame at a time), into the
picture library's "real-questions" folder.
How it is scored: each answer is short (the model is asked for a word or phrase). Accuracy is VizWiz's standard
score, from 0 to 1: an answer that three or more of the ten sighted people also gave scores 1, one that two gave
about 0.6, one that one gave about 0.3. For ORBIT, the answer scores 1 when it names the thing. When most people
said the photo does not show enough, the right answer is "unanswerable" or "I can't tell", and the report says how
often the model said so when it should, and how often it gave up on a question people could answer. Each topic gets
a short line in plain words: good, fair, weak or poor.
How to run it in the program: press "Topic add-ons" (Alt+2), choose the topic in Topic (Alt+T), and press "Test on
real questions" (Alt+Q). It tests that topic's newest finished add-on in the kit's work folder, or the model itself
when there is none yet, on the topic's questions and the everyday ones. The progress page says how it goes; it takes
a few minutes after the first download. Open the report (Alt+E on the progress page) when it says FINISHED: it is
report-real-questions-<name>.txt in the kit folder.
With the .cmd files: kit\run.cmd --evaluate 4b (the model itself), kit\run.cmd --evaluate "<an add-on folder or
result zip>", and add --topic food (or any topic) to test only that topic's questions and the everyday ones.
A topic add-on's training runs this test by itself, before and after, and its report says whether the add-on got
better at the topic's real questions and whether everyday answers stayed as good.

The picture library
The program keeps every picture for good in the picture library folder. It suggests "Picture Library" on the drive
with the most free space on each computer, and you can choose any folder (Browse for the library folder, Alt+L),
also on an external or network drive. It keeps the training copies, full-size copies of every picture (up to 2,048
pixels) for future models, the original downloads and the descriptions with their credits. Pictures prepared before
the library existed are downloaded again at full size the first time. A later training, of this model or a future one, uses them without
downloading anything again. Do not delete it. Pictures an earlier training kept in the kit's work folder are moved
into it the first time the program uses it.

Afterwards
You can delete the "work" folder inside the kit folder to get space back (keep the "result" folder until the file is
sent, and keep "work" until every result zip has its toolkit version: the toolkit version is made from the add-on in
"work"); the picture library stays.
